Chapter one

Designing a data platform

"Design the data platform for a company" is a real interview question and a poorly-taught one, because most answers become a tool list. Apply Book 5's method instead: the layers exist to solve specific problems, and you should be able to say which.

What each layer is actually for

Ingestion
Get data in without coupling to the source's availability or shape. Three modes, and the choice matters: CDC from a database's write-ahead log (complete, low-impact, catches deletes and intermediate states); event streams pushed by producers (fresh, and you own the schema contract); batch pulls from APIs and files (simple, lossy, and it misses anything that changed and changed back). Prefer CDC over polling wherever the source is a database — a scheduled SELECT * WHERE updated_at > ? both loads the production database and silently misses rows that were updated twice between runs.
Landing / raw
Immutable and never edited. This is your source of truth and the thing that makes every downstream mistake recoverable. Store it in the source's shape, not a cleaned-up one — the moment you clean on ingest, you have destroyed your ability to re-derive differently when the definition changes. Retain it long enough to rebuild everything.
Modelling / transformation
Where business meaning is applied: conformed dimensions, de-duplication, joins, agreed definitions. The important property is that it is a pure function of the raw layer. If it is, you can rebuild it; if it is not — because it reads its own previous output, or calls an API whose answer changes — you have created something you cannot reproduce, and you will regret it during your first incident.
Serving
Shaped for consumption: marts for BI, rollups for dashboards, features for models, and reverse-ETL pushing back into operational tools. Each consumer gets a shape, and none of them read the modelling layer directly.

The three things that make it a platform rather than a pile of jobs

This is what interviewers are actually probing when they ask this question.

  • Orchestration with real dependencies. Not cron. A DAG that knows what depends on what, so a late upstream delays its dependents rather than letting them compute on stale inputs and silently produce wrong numbers. The failure mode cron cannot prevent — job B ran at 3am as scheduled, on data job A had not yet produced — is the whole argument.
  • Data contracts. A schema, an owner, a freshness promise, and a change process, agreed between producer and consumer. Without them, a backend engineer renames a column on Tuesday and your pipeline breaks on Wednesday, and the argument about whose fault it is takes longer than the fix. With them, the schema registry rejects the incompatible change at the source. This is the single highest-leverage thing in a data platform and the one most often missing.
  • Observability, specific to data. Not "did the job run" but "is the data right": freshness, volume anomalies, null rates, distribution drift, and referential checks. A pipeline that runs successfully every day while producing garbage is the characteristic failure of this domain, and it is invisible to ordinary monitoring.
The question to be ready for

"A dashboard number is wrong. Walk me through what you do." The answer they want is a procedure, not a guess: check freshness first (is this stale rather than wrong?), then volume against expectation, then trace lineage upstream to find the first layer where the number diverges, then check whether the definition changed rather than the data. Being able to say "and this is why I want column-level lineage and a definition history" turns a debugging story into a platform argument.

Chapter two

Pipelines that survive contact with reality

You know most of this. It is here because in a design round you will be expected to state the properties explicitly rather than just having them, and the words matter.

Idempotent
Re-running a task for a given window produces the same result — no duplicates, no double-counting. Achieved by making the write a replace of a partition rather than an append, or by deduplicating on a natural key. This is the property that makes retries and backfills safe, and it is the same property Book 4 needed for message consumers.
Incremental
Process only what changed, but be able to reprocess everything. The tension is real: incremental logic that cannot be run in full is a trap, because eventually a definition changes and you need to. Keep the full-refresh path working and test it.
Partitioned by processing window
Each run owns one time window and writes only that partition. This is what makes a backfill a parallel operation over windows rather than one enormous serial job, and it is what makes a partial failure recoverable.
Late-data aware
The window closed but data arrived. Decide, explicitly: drop it, hold a grace period and restate, or maintain a correction path. All three are legitimate; leaving it undecided is not, because the answer silently becomes "whatever the code happens to do."
Schema-evolving
Additive changes should be free; breaking changes should be caught at the boundary. The expand-then-contract pattern from Book 3 applies exactly: add the new field, write both, migrate consumers, drop the old.
Tested
Not only unit tests on logic — assertions on the data: uniqueness of keys, referential integrity, accepted ranges, row-count deltas within expectation. These are your production alerts as much as your tests.

The backfill, treated as a first-class design concern

Backfills are where data platforms actually break, and it is worth designing for them rather than improvising each time. The questions to have answered in advance: How long does a full rebuild take? If the answer is "three weeks", you have a serious problem hiding in plain sight. Can it run in parallel with normal operation, or does it contend for the same cluster and the same tables? Does it produce the same result as the incremental path? — a genuinely common bug, where incremental and full-refresh logic have quietly diverged. And can it be resumed after failing 60% of the way through?

The clean answer to all four is the partition discipline above: if each window is independent and idempotent, a backfill is a parallel map over windows that can be re-run at any granularity. Everything else is a workaround.

Chapter three

Storage design questions you will actually be asked

Four decisions, each with a defensible answer, each frequently probed.

1Partitioning strategy

Partition on the column that appears in nearly every query's filter — usually a date. The failure mode is over-partitioning: partitioning by (date, country, device) produces millions of tiny files, and metadata operations become slower than the scan you were avoiding. The rule of thumb worth stating is that a partition should hold at least a few hundred megabytes. Within a partition, use clustering or sort order on the next-most-filtered column so that file-level min/max statistics let the engine skip files. Partitioning is coarse pruning; clustering is fine pruning.

2File format and file size

Columnar (Parquet or ORC) for anything analytical, for the compression and pruning reasons in Book 3. Target files of roughly 128 MB to 1 GB — small enough to parallelize, large enough that per-file overhead and metadata do not dominate. And run compaction, because streaming ingest produces small files by construction. This is an LSM tree's compaction problem, and saying so connects your daily work to the storage-engine chapter.

3Table format — and why it exists

Plain files on object storage have no atomicity: a reader can see a half-written directory, and there is no way to change a schema safely. Iceberg and Delta add a metadata layer that makes a commit atomic (readers see the old snapshot until the new one is committed), gives you schema evolution, time travel, and hidden partitioning. In the vocabulary of this series: they add MVCC and a write-ahead log to a filesystem. If you can say that sentence, you have understood both halves.

4Where the compute lives, and what it costs

Separating storage from compute is the defining architectural choice of the modern warehouse: storage is cheap and permanent, compute is expensive and ephemeral, and you scale them independently. The cost conversation that follows is one you should be able to have — scan cost is driven by bytes read, so partitioning and clustering are cost decisions as much as performance ones; and the honest answer to "how would you halve this bill" is usually a mix of better pruning, tiering old partitions to colder storage, and materializing the five queries that account for most of the scanning.

"What does this cost, and what's the cheapest 10% regression that halves it?" is a standard senior follow-up. Have a shape of an answer.
Chapter four

What makes an ML system different: two extra loops

An ML system is an ordinary system with two additions. Miss either and your design reads as "knows models, hasn't shipped one."

The training loop turns data into models. The feedback loop turns the model's own output into tomorrow's training data — and that second arrow is where everything difficult lives.

The framing step, which is where most answers go wrong

Before any architecture, translate the product goal into an ML problem, and be concrete about four things: what is the input, what is the output, what is one training example, and where does the label come from? "Recommend videos" is not an ML problem. "Given (user, candidate video, context), predict the probability of a watch longer than 30 seconds" is.

Then three tiers of metric, and say all three: the business metric (retention, revenue), the online metric the A/B test measures (watch time, click-through), and the offline metric you optimize daily (AUC, NDCG). Plus explicit guardrails you must not regress. The interesting engineering conversation is usually about the gaps between the tiers, because they diverge constantly.

The label question, which people skip

Where does ground truth come from and how long until you have it? Implicit feedback (clicks, watches) is free, abundant, and biased. Explicit feedback (ratings, human review) is clean and scarce. And the delay matters enormously to the design: fraud labels arrive 30 days later via chargebacks, which means your model is always being evaluated on a month-old world and your retraining cadence cannot outrun your labels.

Chapter five

Features, and the two bugs that define this field

Feature engineering is a data engineering problem wearing a data science hat, which puts it squarely in your lane. Two specific failures account for most production ML disappointment, and both are pipeline bugs rather than modelling bugs.

Bug one — training/serving skew

The feature is computed one way in a Spark job for training and another way in the serving path — a different default for nulls, a different time window, a different rounding, a subtly different join. The model was trained on one world and predicts in another, and the metric quietly degrades with no error anywhere.

The fix is architectural, not disciplinary: one feature definition, compiled to both paths. That is the primary reason feature stores exist. Plus a continuous check that compares features logged at serving time against features recomputed from the warehouse for the same entity and timestamp — if they diverge, something is wrong and you want to know before the metric does.

Bug two — label leakage, via time

A feature encodes something that was not knowable at prediction time. The obvious version is easy to spot ("items in cart at checkout" predicting purchase). The insidious version is a time-travel bug in a join: you compute "user's average order value" over the whole table, including orders placed after the event you are training on. Offline metrics look wonderful; production is a disappointment.

The fix has a name and it is worth knowing: point-in-time correct joins — every feature value is taken as of the prediction timestamp, never later. Doing this correctly by hand in SQL is genuinely difficult, which is the second reason feature stores exist. If you have written an as-of join with a window function and been careful about the inequality, you have already done this and can say so.

What a feature store actually is

Strip the marketing and it is three things: an offline store (columnar, for building training sets with point-in-time correctness), an online store (key-value, for low-latency lookup at serving time), and a registry of definitions shared across teams, with freshness guarantees. Batch pipelines and streaming pipelines write both.

The honest assessment, which is a good thing to volunteer: it is overhead for one model and essential at ten. The value is not the storage — it is having one definition of "user's 7-day purchase count" instead of five slightly different ones in five notebooks.

Feature freshness as a design axis

Group features by how fresh they must be, because that determines the pipeline: batch (user's lifetime spend — recomputed nightly, cheap), streaming (items viewed in this session — a windowed aggregation, seconds fresh), and request-time (the query itself, the current page). Most systems need all three, and the streaming ones are both the most valuable and the most likely to skew. Being able to place each feature in one of those three buckets, with a reason, is a strong answer to "how would you build these features?"

Chapter six

Serving a model

First question: does this need to be real-time at all?

Ask what the prediction depends on. If it depends only on things known in advance — user history, item catalogue — and the set of inputs is enumerable, precompute in batch and serve a lookup. Orders of magnitude cheaper, trivially cacheable, and it cannot time out. Only if the prediction depends on the request itself — the query, the session, the candidate set — does it have to be online.

Most real systems are hybrid, and describing the split is the good answer: embeddings computed nightly in batch, the interaction scored in real time. There is also a middle option people forget — near-real-time, where a streaming job maintains counters and short-window aggregates — which covers most "but it needs fresh data" objections at a fraction of the cost.

The funnel, and why it is forced

Suppose you must rank a billion candidates in 200 milliseconds, and a good ranking model costs a millisecond per item. A billion milliseconds is eleven days. So the architecture is not a choice: cheap filters over billions, expensive models over hundreds, with each stage cutting the set by roughly 100× and affording roughly 100× more compute per item.

Judge each stage by its own metric — retrieval by recall@k, ranking by NDCG. Judging a retriever on precision is a category error: its only job is to not lose the good item.

The staircase to production

Being able to name this sequence is one of the strongest shipping signals available: offline evaluation on a time-based split (never a random split — that leaks the future) → shadow mode, scoring real traffic with outputs logged but not shown → canary at 1% → A/B test sized by a power calculation → ramp, with automatic rollback wired to guardrail metrics. And a model registry so that "roll back to the previous model" is one command rather than an afternoon.

Monitoring, when labels arrive late

You often cannot measure accuracy in real time, so watch the things that need no labels: feature drift (distribution shift per feature against training), prediction drift (your own score distribution moving — the earliest cheap signal), and data quality (null rates, cardinality, an upstream schema change). Most "model degradation" is in fact an upstream pipeline that changed a unit or started emitting nulls, which is a data engineering incident wearing an ML costume.

And distinguish data drift (inputs moved) from concept drift (the relationship moved). The first may be harmless; the second usually requires retraining.

The loop that eats itself

You only get labels for what you showed. The model recommends what it already believes is good, receives confirming clicks, and narrows — while a genuinely better item that never got shown never gets a chance to prove it. This is the feedback loop, and it is the failure mode with no analogue in ordinary systems.

The mitigations: log the propensity of each impression so you can reweight, reserve a small slice of randomized exposure as unbiased evaluation data, and build deliberate exploration into re-ranking. Naming this unprompted is one of the clearest markers of someone who has run a production model rather than trained one.

Chapter seven

An ML design, compressed

Same seven steps from Book 5, with the training and feedback loops folded in. Prompt: "Design a system that detects fraudulent transactions."

StepThe answer
1Scope and framing. Given a transaction and its context, predict the probability it is fraudulent, in time to block it. Non-functional: a decision in under 100 ms inside the payment flow, because the user is waiting; must degrade to a rules-only path if the model service is down; every decision must be explainable to a human reviewer for dispute handling. One training example is one transaction with its point-in-time features and its eventual label.
2Estimate. 10,000 transactions/s at peak; fraud rate ~0.1%, so ~10 positives/s. That imbalance is the design constraint: accuracy is a useless metric, and you will optimize PR-AUC and pick a threshold from a cost matrix rather than at 0.5.
3Interface. POST /score with the transaction and returns {score, decision, reasons[], model_version} — the reasons and the version are not decoration, they are what makes disputes and rollbacks possible.
4State. Transactions in the transactional database (source of truth). Features split by freshness: batch (account age, historical volumes), streaming (transactions in the last 5 minutes on this card, distinct devices this hour — these carry most of the signal), request-time (amount, merchant, geo). Labels arrive up to 30 days later from chargebacks and land in the warehouse. Model artifacts in a registry. Graph features — shared devices, IPs, payment instruments — need a store that supports multi-hop lookup, and are frequently the strongest signal.
5Paths. Score path: request → feature fetch from the online store (one batched multi-get, not N lookups) → rules engine and model in parallel → combine → decide → log everything including the exact feature values used, which is what makes the skew check possible. Training path: warehouse → point-in-time correct join of features to labels → train → evaluate on a time-based split → register → shadow → canary → ramp.
6Bottleneck: the 100 ms budget and the label delay. On latency — feature fetch dominates, so batch the lookups, keep the online store in memory, and cap the streaming aggregate window. On labels — you cannot wait 30 days to know if today's model works, so you need proxy signals (manual review outcomes, which arrive in hours) plus a delayed true evaluation, and you must design the retraining cadence around the label horizon rather than around a convenient schedule. And adversaries adapt: fraud is the one domain where the data distribution actively fights back, which makes drift monitoring a requirement rather than a nicety.
7Operate. Monitor score distribution, feature drift, review-queue volume, and the false-positive rate — because a blocked good customer costs more than most fraud. Degraded mode: rules-only, with a deliberately conservative threshold, and say that out loud. Also design the human review queue as part of the system: it is sized by capacity, it is where your highest-quality labels come from, and its output feeds back into training.

Notice how little of that was about the model. Two sentences on modelling; everything else was data freshness, latency budgets, label timing, feedback, and failure. That ratio is correct, and it is the thing most candidates get wrong in the other direction.

The three things that will actually move you forward now

1Redraw, don't reread

Take the event analytics design from Book 5, close the page, and rebuild it on paper — out loud. Then diff. Whatever is missing from your version is what you actually need to study, and it will be a much shorter list than the whole series. Rereading feels productive and builds recognition rather than recall; the blank page is the only honest test.

2Re-derive a system you already work on

Take a pipeline or platform you own and run the seven steps on it as though you were designing it fresh. You will find decisions that were never decided, state whose consistency requirement nobody named, and at least one component that exists for reasons that stopped being true. This is the single most useful exercise in the series, because you can check your reasoning against something real.

3Practise the narration, not the knowledge

The gap between knowing a design and presenting one is larger than it looks, and it only closes by speaking. Pick a prompt, set 45 minutes, record yourself, and listen back at 1.5×. It is uncomfortable and it is the fastest feedback available — you will hear the tangents, the unstated assumptions, and the places where you had the right answer and never said it.

The mouth is the muscle being trained. The first time you narrate a design should not be in an interview.

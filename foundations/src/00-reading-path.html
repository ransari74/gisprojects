Foundations · The reading path

Six books, in order.

A complete read rather than a reference — each book is a separate page you can finish in one sitting, and they build. The premise throughout is that you can already code and have already run distributed systems; what you are missing is the vocabulary those systems are discussed in and a repeatable method for designing one from nothing.

~4.5 hours totalSix standalone pagesRead in order

Why this series exists

Standard system design material has a hidden assumption: that you build web APIs. It opens by explaining caching to someone who has never had a latency problem, spends chapters on REST semantics, and treats a Kafka topic as an exotic component rather than as the thing you read from every day.

If you come from data engineering or data science, that framing wastes your existing knowledge and hides the actual gaps. You have partitioned datasets, debugged skew, made tasks idempotent, run backfills, and reasoned about late-arriving data. Those are not adjacent skills — they are the same problems that distributed systems textbooks describe under different names. So this series starts by translating what you have, then fills in what is genuinely missing, and only then gets to method.

The three gaps it targets, in order of how much they cost you:

  1. A procedure for designing something from nothing. Not a catalogue of components — an ordered set of questions where each answer constrains the next, so the components fall out at the end rather than being chosen at the start. That is Book 5.
  2. Depth on the parts a framework was hiding. What a server physically is, what a database does underneath the SQL, and what happens on the network. Books 2 through 4.
  3. Knowing what to do with a component you can already define. Knowing what Redis is gets you nothing; knowing what it fixes, what it costs, how it fails, and what smaller thing would have worked instead is the whole game. Book 1 gives you that frame and the rest use it.

01

From Pipelines to Systems

A translation table from what you already do to what it is called on the other side — then the one difference that genuinely does not transfer, and why every hard problem in distributed systems is a question about state.

Sharding ≡ partitioning · hot keys ≡ skew · caching ≡ broadcast joins · WAL ≡ checkpointing · replay ≡ backfill · LSM compaction ≡ the small files problem · MVCC ≡ Iceberg snapshots · the four-part component card · the seven-step method previewed

02

What a Server Is

The machine underneath, drawn to human scale, so that capacity questions become arithmetic instead of guesswork. Short, and everything after it stands on this.

03

How Data Is Stored

Builds a database from an empty file, hitting one wall at a time, so that every storage feature arrives as the solution to a specific failure rather than as a fact to memorize.

Where to start, depending on you

In order is best. But if you are short on time or already solid somewhere:

"I need the method, quickly"

1 → 5. Book 1 gives you the frame and the vocabulary; Book 5 gives you the procedure and two worked examples. About 95 minutes, and it is the highest-value pair in the series. Come back for 2–4 when a specific gap bites.

"I'm shaky on what's under the SQL"

2 → 3. The machine, then the database built from an empty file. These two together are where most of the "I know the word but not the mechanism" feeling lives, and they fix it permanently.

"I'm interviewing for an ML role"

1 → 3 → 5 → 6. Skip Book 4 initially if you must. An ML design round is a distributed systems round with two extra loops, so Books 3 and 5 matter more than people expect, and Book 6 is the round itself.

"I want the complete read"

1 → 6, in order, at roughly one book per sitting over a week. Each ends by pointing at the next. After each, close the page and redraw its central diagram from memory — that fifteen minutes is worth more than the reading.

How to actually retain this

Reading a design is worth about a fifth of drawing one. After each book, spend fifteen minutes at a blank page rebuilding its main idea from memory, speaking it out loud, then diff against the text. The diff is your real study list, and it will be far shorter than the book. This is the only instruction in the series that changes outcomes on its own.

The companion drill book

This series teaches. There is a separate, larger reference built for practice — a 22-section interview book with a 28-day schedule, 175 pattern-mapped LeetCode problems as a tracker, twelve worked designs, LLM and agent systems, mock rubrics, and company-by-company loop structure.

They are complementary, and the order matters: read the foundations first, drill second. Drilling before you have the model just makes the gaps harder to see.

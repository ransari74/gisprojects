Section 20 · The loop

Company by company

The loops differ more than prep guides admit, and the differences change what you should practice. They also change faster than any document can track — so this section marks what is published by the companies themselves, what is well-attested practitioner knowledge, and what is rumor.

Read this before you plan around anything below

The interview-prep web is now heavily polluted with generated SEO content that invents specific-sounding round counts, minute-by-minute breakdowns, and rejection statistics. Treat any unsourced precision with suspicion — including here. Everything below is marked: published by the company, reported by mainstream journalism, or practitioner consensus from prep firms and ex-interviewers. Every ambiguity in this section is resolvable by one email to your recruiter, and that email beats every guide on the internet, this one included.

Amazon's ladder is offset by one number. Amazon L5 is mid-level and maps to Meta E4 / Google L4; Amazon L6 is senior and maps to E5 / L5. Candidates who see "L6" on an Amazon offer and compare it to a Google L6 are making an expensive error. Cross-company mapping is genuinely contested at the edges — Amazon L6 often carries tech-lead scope that Meta and Google put at E6/L6 — so treat any mapping table, including this one, as approximate.

What changes between mid and senior — in the design round

This is the highest-value content in the section, because a shallow, interviewer-led design round is the single most common downlevel path at Google, Meta, Amazon and Microsoft alike. Six concrete differences:

  1. Who drives

    Mid: the interviewer asks, you answer. Senior: you set the agenda and propose what to go deep on. Waiting to be prompted is itself the failing signal.

  2. Requirements

    Mid: takes the prompt at face value. Senior: scopes it — QPS, read/write ratio, latency budget, consistency needs, explicit non-goals — before drawing any boxes.

  3. Depth selection

    Senior candidates identify the hardest part unprompted and go deep on two or three components, instead of spreading evenly across ten.

  4. Failure modes, not just tradeoffs

    Mid: "I'd use Kafka because it's durable." Senior: "here's what breaks under this failure, here's the blast radius, here's how it degrades." Staff: "here's how this design ages over three years and what forces the migration."

  5. Operational reality

    Senior and above are expected to cover deployment, monitoring and alerting, rollout and rollback, capacity, and on-call burden. Mid-level is not.

  6. Staff-only axes

    Platform boundaries and team ownership, incremental migration paths, build-versus-buy, cost, and standardization against team autonomy.

How the decision is actually made

Most candidates believe the model is "score well, get hired." The real mechanics are different in ways that should change your behavior:

  • Each interviewer submits an independent written recommendation — plus a level recommendation — before seeing anyone else's. You are not being scored live; you are being written down.
  • A committee or debrief reads packets, not people. Your interviewer's ability to reconstruct your reasoning an hour later matters more than the reasoning felt in the room. This is the reframing that improves every other piece of advice in this guide: make it easy to write you up well. Label your structure out loud ("three functional requirements, then two non-functional"), state tradeoffs as sentences, and summarize at the end.
  • One strong no-hire usually sinks a loop, regardless of the other four.
  • Downleveling is a routine outcome, not a failure. The same performance is a hire at one level and a no-hire at the next.
  • Cooldowns of roughly 6–12 months apply after a rejection; Google publishes a 90-day wait for the same role, and notes that reapplying candidates do best with 12–18 months more experience.
  • Passing the committee is not an offer at Google — it gets you into team matching, which commonly adds weeks and occasionally doesn't resolve at all.

The AI-tool question — and companies are moving in opposite directions

This is the biggest recent change to these loops, and getting it wrong is disqualifying. Ask your recruiter, in writing, for each round.

CompanyDirectionWhat's actually reported
MetaUse it, and be graded on itreported An AI-enabled coding round replaces one of the two onsite coding interviews — roughly 60 minutes, in an environment with a built-in assistant. Meta's internal framing is that it is more representative of real work and makes LLM-based cheating less effective. Candidate reports describe a bug-fix → implement → optimize shape; that structure is consistent across sources but is not an official spec.
GooglePiloting the same directionreported A pilot round where candidates read, debug and optimize an existing codebase with an assistant available, evaluating "AI fluency" — prompting, validating output, debugging. Reported as junior-to-mid roles on select US teams. Separately, Google's CEO has said publicly they will introduce at least one in-person round to verify fundamentals; that is a stated intention, not a documented rollout, so don't assume your onsite is in person.
AmazonBanned, and disqualifyingreported Amazon's position is that real-time AI assistance gives an unfair advantage that masks authentic ability; candidates acknowledge they will not use unauthorized tools during interviews or assessments.
AnthropicPublished, and unusually precisepublished Applications: write your own first draft, then use AI to refine — "use AI to refine ideas, not replace them." Live interviews: not permitted unless explicitly stated, because they want to see real-time reasoning. Take-homes: complete without AI unless told otherwise — and they say so explicitly when it is allowed.
Apple, MicrosoftUnknownNo primary sourcing exists either way. Treat as "ask", not as "permitted".
The most interesting artifact in this whole space

Anthropic has published that its performance-optimization take-home has had to be redesigned with each new model release, because successive Claude models first matched and then exceeded most human applicants under the same time limit — on a take-home where AI use is expressly permitted. The problem isn't cheating; it's that if humans can't improve on the model's output, the test measures which model you used rather than who you are. If you want to understand what an AI-era technical evaluation is trying to measure, that write-up is the clearest public statement of it — and it tells you what to practice: judgment about model output, not speed of typing.

Amazon's Leadership Principles

There are 16 (14 originals plus two added in 2021). They are not trivia — they are the literal rubric, each interviewer is assigned two or three, and a technically strong LP-weak candidate fails at Amazon in a way they would not at Google. Read the official wording on Amazon's own site; the gists below are for mapping your stories, not for quoting.

The ones that carry most weight in engineering loops
  • Customer Obsession — start from the customer and work backwards.
  • Ownership — long-term thinking; never "that's not my job."
  • Invent and Simplify — find the simpler thing; look outside for ideas.
  • Dive Deep — stay in the details, audit, be skeptical when metrics and anecdote disagree.
  • Bias for Action — most decisions are reversible; calculated risk beats delay.
  • Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit — challenge respectfully, then commit fully once decided.
  • Deliver Results — focus on key inputs; never settle after a setback.
  • Insist on the Highest Standards — don't send defects down the line.
The rest, which still get asked
  • Are Right, A Lot — strong judgment; actively seek to disconfirm your own beliefs.
  • Learn and Be Curious
  • Hire and Develop the Best — raise the bar with every hire; coach others.
  • Think Big
  • Frugality — constraints breed resourcefulness.
  • Earn Trust — listen, speak candidly, be vocally self-critical.
  • Strive to be Earth's Best Employer
  • Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility

The work is the mapping. Take the eight stories from §18 and build a grid: story down one axis, LPs across the other, and make sure every principle in the left-hand column above is covered by at least two stories. Amazon expects STAR, expects quantified results, and will drill: what was the data? what did you do? what would you do differently? Prepare the follow-ups, not just the story.

If you're targeting an ML or AI role

The loop grows a fifth signal area. Coding is still tested at close to full SWE difficulty — "I do modeling, not engineering" no longer passes anywhere. On top of that: an ML breadth round (fundamentals and theory), the ML system design round (§14–15), and a project deep-dive where you walk through a system you actually built — why that model, what the baseline was, how you evaluated, what went wrong in production. That last round is where inflated ML experience comes apart.

  • ML platform / infra roles ask about serving systems, dynamic batching, KV caching, quantization, GPU scheduling, and distributed training — the most commonly reported prompt is some form of "design a batch inference API for a GPU cluster", which is really a queueing and resource-management question.
  • Applied-AI roles test LLM fundamentals, context engineering, RAG and vector databases, agents (tool use, orchestration, memory, guardrails), and production operations — latency budgets, token cost, routing, evals.
  • Research engineer loops add a paper deep-dive with an actual researcher, experiment design ("how would you test that? what's the ablation?"), and — the round that most distinguishes RE from SWE — debugging a training run: the loss spiked, or it's flat, or eval diverges from train; what do you check, in what order.

Two scheduling levers most candidates don't use. First, recruiters will almost always grant more prep time if you ask up front with a reason — the default two-to-four-week gap is a convention, not a constraint. Second, do not schedule your top-choice company first. Run a lower-stakes loop as calibration: the formats are similar enough that one real onsite is worth ten mocks, and you only get to be nervous-for-the-first-time once.

The email to send before you start prepping

"Before I plan my preparation — could you confirm: which rounds will I have and how long is each? What level am I being considered at, and is the loop level-flexible? Is any round in person? And what's your policy on AI tool use in each round?" Recruiters answer this routinely. It resolves nearly every ambiguity in this section, and the answer is worth more than anything you'll read about their process online.

Section 18 · The loop

Behavioral & the story bank

At senior level this round is not a formality — it is where scope, ownership, and influence get calibrated, and it is the round most often failed by people who prepared only for the technical ones. The fix is small: eight stories, written down, said out loud until they're two minutes long.

Build the bank once, reuse it everywhere

Interviewers ask maybe thirty different questions, but they map onto a handful of themes. Write eight stories, each reusable for three or four questions. Check them off as you write and rehearse each one:

STAR, with the part everyone forgets

BeatTimeContains
Situation15 sJust enough context. Team size, what the system did, what was at stake. Not a company history.
Task15 sYour specific responsibility. "I owned" beats "we needed to".
Action60 sThe bulk. Use I for decisions and judgment calls, we for execution — an unbroken "I" through a story that obviously took a team reads as credit-taking, which is its own downgrade at senior level. Name who else was involved and what you needed from them. Include the option you rejected and why.
Result20 sA number. p99 from 800 ms to 120 ms; cost down 40%; two-week release cycle to daily.
Learning10 sThe beat most people skip. "What I'd do differently is instrument before optimizing — I spent a week on the wrong bottleneck."
The 90-second "tell me about yourself"

Four sentences: where you are now and what you own · the through-line of your last few years, framed as a specialty rather than a list of jobs · one concrete accomplishment with a number · why this team, specifically. Then stop and let them ask. Rehearse it verbatim — it is the only answer you can safely memorize, and it sets the frame for the entire loop.

What "senior" sounds like

Scope

Mid-level stories end at "I built it". Senior stories include who else was affected, what you had to negotiate, and what you chose not to build.

Ownership

You noticed the problem before you were assigned it. You stayed with it through the boring operational tail, not just the interesting build.

Judgment

You can articulate the option you didn't take, and you're honest about what your choice cost. Certainty about everything reads as inexperience.

Impact

Every story lands on a measurable outcome — and you know the number because you set up the measurement.

Ask them something real

The questions you ask are scored. Skip anything answerable from the careers page. These work because they require a specific answer: "What does the on-call rotation actually look like — how many pages a week?" · "What's the biggest piece of technical debt this team is carrying, and is there a plan?" · "How does a model or feature get from an idea to production here — who has to say yes?" · "What separates someone doing well on this team from someone doing exceptionally well?"

One to use carefully: "What made the last person who left this team decide to?" is a genuinely revealing question for a hiring manager, and a risky one for a peer interviewer — many are instructed not to discuss attrition, and it can turn a rapport moment defensive. Ask it of the manager, not the panel.

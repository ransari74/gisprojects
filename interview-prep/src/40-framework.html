Section 09 · System design

The framework

A design interview is not a test of whether you know what Kafka is. It is a test of whether you can take an under-specified problem, make the ambiguity explicit, and drive to a defensible design while thinking out loud. The structure below is what stops you from wandering.

The seven steps, on a clock

Assume 45 minutes of design time. Write these on your scratch page before the interviewer finishes the prompt — visible structure is itself a signal.

MinStepWhat you produceThe failure if you skip it
0–5Scope3–5 functional requirements, 3 non-functional ones, and an explicit out of scope list.You design the wrong system beautifully.
5–10ScaleDAU → QPS (average and peak), storage per year, bandwidth. Round aggressively.Every later choice is unjustified. "Why shard?" has no answer.
10–13API4–6 endpoints with the actual parameters, and the pagination token.The data model floats free of any caller.
13–18DataEntities, access patterns, the store per entity, and the partition key.You pick a database by vibes. This is the most common senior-level miss.
18–28ArchitectureThe box diagram: client → edge → services → stores, plus the async path.Nothing to deep-dive into.
28–40Deep diveOne or two bottlenecks taken to the bottom. Name the alternatives you rejected.This is where the level is decided. A shallow dive caps you at mid-level.
40–45WrapFailure modes, what you'd monitor, and the single tradeoff you are least comfortable with.You end mid-sentence and it reads as incomplete.

Step 1 in practice: the scoping questions that pay

Ask five, not fifteen. These five almost always change the design:

  1. Read or write heavy, and by what ratio? A 100:1 read ratio means caching and read replicas; 1:1 means the write path is the design.
  2. How stale can this be? Seconds of staleness unlocks async fan-out, denormalization, and caches. "Must be immediately consistent" removes all three.
  3. What is the latency target, at what percentile? p50 is a vanity number. Design against p99.
  4. How big is the largest entity, and the largest fan-out? The celebrity with 100M followers is the entire reason feed systems are interesting.
  5. Global or single region? Multi-region turns every consistency question into a hard one.
Phrasing that buys signal
  • "I'll assume X — tell me if that's wrong." Beats silence, and beats asking permission for every assumption.
  • "There are three ways to do this. I'll take B because of the write ratio; A wins if reads dominate." Naming the loser is the signal.
  • "That's a consistency question — let me finish the write path and come back to it in two minutes." Interviewers reward a driver.
  • "This is where I'd want a real number. If it's under 10k QPS, one Postgres box does it; over that, I shard."
Six ways candidates lose the round
  • Boxes with no arrows-with-nouns. Every arrow needs a protocol and a payload, or it's decoration.
  • Buzzword stacking. Naming Kafka, Redis, Cassandra and Spark in one breath without a reason each. Expect "why not just Postgres?" — and have the answer.
  • No numbers. Sharding without ever computing that the data doesn't fit on one machine.
  • Designing for 100× the stated scale. Over-engineering reads as poor judgment, not ambition.
  • Going silent while thinking. A few seconds is fine and normal. A long unexplained silence isn't — not because a stopwatch is running, but because the interviewer can't write down what they can't hear. "Let me think about the write path for a moment" costs nothing and buys all the silence you need.
  • Defending a choice past its evidence. When the interviewer pushes twice, they are usually handing you something. Take it.

What is actually on the scorecard

Problem navigation

Did you scope, prioritize, and drive — or wait to be steered? Senior candidates run the clock themselves.

Technical depth

One component taken to the bottom: the data structure, the failure mode, the actual bytes. Breadth without a dive reads junior.

Tradeoff reasoning

Alternatives named and rejected for a stated reason tied to the requirements. Companies weight their axes differently and none publish the weights — but this is the axis that most reliably distinguishes a senior write-up from a mid-level one, because it is the part an interviewer can quote back an hour later.

Communication

Could a peer follow your diagram without you? Did you check in before long tangents?

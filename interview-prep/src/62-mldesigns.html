Section 15 · AI & ML

Four worked ML designs

Two classical (ranking and retrieval) and two that now show up in nearly every loop (RAG and LLM serving). The pattern to internalize from the first two is multi-stage funnels; from the second two, memory and batching arithmetic.

Design 5 — Video recommendation feed

Prompt: rank a personalized home feed over a catalog of 1 billion videos, 500M DAU, p99 under 200 ms.

Why a funnel, in one sentence of arithmetic

A good ranking model costs ~1 ms per item. Scoring 1 billion candidates per request is 106 seconds. You have 200 ms. So the design is forced: cheap filters over billions, expensive models over hundreds — each stage cuts the candidate set by ~100× and can afford ~100× more compute per item.

Each stage optimizes a different thing: retrieval maximizes recall, ranking maximizes precision at the top, re-ranking optimizes the set rather than the items.

Retrieval: blend several sources, don't build one perfect one

  • Two-tower embedding retrieval — a user tower and an item tower trained so that dot(u, v) is high for engaged pairs. Item embeddings are precomputed into an ANN index; the user embedding is computed per request. This is the workhorse.
  • Collaborative signals — "users who watched X also watched Y", precomputed offline.
  • Trending / fresh — a small, always-on slice so new content can ever be discovered.
  • Followed creators — a straight lookup, no model.

Union the sources, dedupe, and let ranking sort it out. The interesting part is negative sampling: train with in-batch negatives (other items in the same batch, essentially free) plus hard negatives (items shown but not clicked). Train only on positives and the model learns that everything is great.

Ranking: it is multi-task, and the weights are a product decision

A single "will they click" head optimizes for clickbait. Real systems predict several heads from one shared trunk — P(click), P(watch > 30s), P(complete), P(like), P(hide) — and combine them:

The scoring function is where product strategy actually lives
score = (w1*p_click + w2*p_watch30 + w3*p_complete + w4*p_like
         - w5*p_hide - w6*p_report)
        * quality_prior(video)
        * freshness_decay(age)

# Say this out loud: the weights are not learned end-to-end, they are
# tuned against the A/B guardrails. Learning them from engagement alone
# is how a feed optimizes itself into outrage. Naming that tradeoff is
# a strong senior signal — it is a values question, not a modeling one.

Features, grouped the way you should say them: user (long-term embedding, watch history, demographics), item (embedding, age, creator, historical CTR — smoothed, or new items look terrible), cross (has the user watched this creator, topic affinity, similarity of user and item embeddings), context (hour, device, network speed, session depth). Real-time counters ("views in the last 5 minutes") come from a streaming aggregation, and they are the features most likely to skew between training and serving.

Re-ranking and the cold-start question

Never ship the raw sorted list. Apply diversity (cap items per creator or topic — MMR, or a simple greedy per-slot constraint), dedupe against recent impressions, and exploration: a small epsilon of random or Thompson-sampled items so the system keeps learning and new content has a path to discovery. For cold-start users, fall back to popularity within their coarse segment and update on the first few interactions; for cold-start items, rely on content embeddings (title, thumbnail, audio, creator prior) rather than behavioral signals, and give a deliberate exploration budget of impressions to gather them.

Design 6 — Search ranking with embeddings

Prompt: build search over 10 billion documents — return the 10 best results in under 300 ms.

Same funnel, different retrieval. Two complementary retrieval paths, and the right answer is hybrid:

PathMechanismStrong atWeak at
Lexical (BM25 over an inverted index)term → posting list, scored by term frequency and inverse document frequencyExact terms, rare words, product codes, names. Cheap, explainable, no training.Synonyms and paraphrase. "cheap flights" misses "affordable airfare".
Semantic (dense vectors + ANN)encode query and documents into the same space, nearest-neighbor searchMeaning, paraphrase, cross-lingual, "that movie with the spinning top".Exact identifiers. Vector search happily returns something similar to a part number that is not it.

Run both, then fuse — reciprocal rank fusion (Σ 1/(k + rank_i), k≈60) needs no score calibration between the two systems and is what you should name. Then a cross-encoder re-ranker scores the top ~100: it reads query and document together so it can model interaction, which is far more accurate and far too slow to run over more than a couple hundred items. That accuracy/cost split — bi-encoder for retrieval, cross-encoder for re-ranking — is the single most useful idea in modern retrieval.

ANN indexes: know the tradeoff, not just the names

IndexIdeaTradeoff
HNSWMulti-layer navigable small-world graph; greedy descent from a sparse top layerBest recall/latency available. Memory hungry (graph plus full vectors) and expensive to update in bulk.
IVF (inverted file)Cluster vectors, search only the nprobe nearest clustersSimple knob for recall vs speed. Recall drops at cluster boundaries.
PQ (product quantization)Split the vector into subvectors, replace each with a codebook id10–30× memory reduction, some recall loss. Usually combined: IVF-PQ.
ScaNN / DiskANNAnisotropic quantization; SSD-resident graphsWhen the index cannot fit in RAM at any price.
The sizing question you will be asked

10B documents × 768-dim float32 = 1010 × 768 × 4 B ≈ 30 TB of raw vectors, so shard by document and scatter-gather. Then reduce, showing each factor separately so the number is reproducible:

StepFactorSizeCost
float32, 768 dims—30 TBbaseline
Truncate to 256 dims (Matryoshka)÷310 TBsmall recall loss; MRL-trained models degrade gracefully
int8 quantization÷42.5 TBminor; recovered by reranking on exact vectors
or product quantization instead÷30~0.9 TBreal recall loss — must over-retrieve and rerank

And then name the term everyone forgets: at 10B vectors an HNSW graph costs roughly M × 2 × 4 bytes of links per vector — at M=16 that's ~128 B/vector, or 1.3 TB of graph, which is larger than the quantized vectors it sits on top of. Optimizing only the vectors optimizes the smaller term. That is also the reason HNSW isn't the right index at this scale at all: 10B vectors is IVF-PQ or DiskANN territory, where the graph or centroids stay in RAM and the vectors live on SSD. Do this arithmetic out loud; it is the whole reason quantization and disk-resident indexes exist.

Design 7 — RAG assistant over company documents

Prompt: an internal assistant that answers questions from 10M documents, with citations, and must not make things up. This is now one of the most common ML design prompts, and the bar is whether you treat it as an information retrieval problem with a generation step — which it is — rather than a prompt engineering problem.

Retrieval quality caps answer quality. If the right chunk is not in the top 8, no model and no prompt can save the answer.

Indexing: chunking is the decision that matters most

  • Chunk on structure, not on character count. Split at headings and paragraphs, target ~500 tokens with ~15% overlap so a sentence spanning a boundary survives in one piece.
  • Keep the parent. Retrieve small chunks for precision, but pass the surrounding section to the model for context — "small-to-big" retrieval.
  • Prepend context to each chunk before embedding — document title, section path, and a one-line summary of what the parent document is about. A chunk reading "it increased 12% year over year" is unretrievable without it.
  • Store metadata for filtering — ACL groups, source, timestamp, language. Post-filtering empties a user's top-k and shows them nothing, so you want to filter first — but be honest that filtered ANN is genuinely hard: pre-filtering an HNSW graph wrecks recall when the filter is selective, because the graph's navigability assumes the full node set. The real options are a partitioned index per ACL group, IVF with per-list filtering, or over-fetching against a measured recall budget. "Pre-filter" is the right instinct and not a free one.
  • Re-embed on model change. Changing the embedding model invalidates the entire index — 10M documents is a real re-indexing job, and planning for it (versioned indexes, dual-write, atomic swap) is a strong operational signal.
Size it, like any other index

10M documents × ~20 chunks each = 200M chunks. At 768 dims float32 that is ~614 GB of vectors plus the graph — comfortably a single beefy machine after int8 quantization, which is a genuinely useful thing to establish early, because it tells you this is not a distributed-systems problem and the effort belongs in retrieval quality instead. Per-query cost is (prompt tokens + output tokens) × price; with 8 chunks of 500 tokens you are paying for ~5k input tokens on every question, which is what makes prefix caching and small-model routing matter.

Grounding, and why "don't hallucinate" is a system property

You cannot prompt a model into being honest; you build a system where unsupported claims are detectable. Layer it:

  1. Require citations per claim — the prompt asks for spans tagged with chunk ids, and a post-processor verifies each cited id was actually retrieved. Hallucinated citations are trivially caught this way.
  2. Verify support — an entailment check (a small NLI model, or a second cheap LLM call) that asks "is this sentence supported by this chunk?" for the highest-stakes claims.
  3. Refuse deliberately — below a confidence threshold, answer "I don't have a document that covers this." But use a reranker score, not raw cosine similarity: embedding scores aren't comparable across queries (the distribution shifts with query length and specificity), so a fixed global cosine threshold over-refuses on some query shapes and under-refuses on others. A calibrated refusal rate is a shipped feature, not a failure.
  4. Show your work — render the source chunks alongside the answer so a human can check in two seconds.

Evaluation, which is where most candidates go vague

Split it in two, because the fixes are different. Retrieval: build a golden set of ~200 question→document pairs (labelled by hand, once, and worth the day it takes) and track recall@k and MRR. Generation: track faithfulness (fraction of claims supported by the retrieved context), answer relevance, and citation precision, scored by an LLM judge that you have calibrated against human labels on a sample. Then online: thumbs, follow-up-question rate, escalation-to-human rate, and time-to-answer. Say the failure taxonomy out loud — retrieval miss, retrieval hit but ignored, hallucinated detail, stale document, permission leak — because each one points to a different part of the pipeline.

Cost and latency: caching is the biggest lever, but be careful what you cache. A naive semantic cache — embed the query, return the cached answer above ~0.97 cosine — is a correctness bug in a system whose stated requirement is not making things up: "did revenue increase in Q3?" and "did revenue decrease in Q3?" sit far above 0.97 in any general embedding space and have opposite answers. Embeddings encode topic, not polarity. Cache the retrieval (safe — you re-generate against the same chunks), and gate any answer cache on a normalized exact-match or a cheap equivalence check. Prefix-caching the long system prompt is free money and has no such hazard. Stream tokens over SSE so time-to-first-token — the number users actually feel — stays under ~500 ms even when the full answer takes 5 s. Route by difficulty: a small model handles the easy majority, a large one handles the rest.

Design 8 — LLM serving platform

Prompt: serve a 70B-parameter model to 10,000 requests per second across many teams. This is an infrastructure question wearing an AI hat, and it is won with arithmetic.

The memory math, which you should be able to do on the whiteboard

Two numbers decide the whole architecture
# 1. WEIGHTS — fixed cost, paid once per GPU replica
70B params x 2 bytes (fp16)  = 140 GB   # > 80 GB card -> tensor-parallel over 2-8 GPUs.

The four techniques to name

  • Continuous (in-flight) batching — the single biggest throughput win. Static batching waits for the slowest sequence in the batch to finish; continuous batching evicts finished sequences and admits new ones every step, typically a multiple-times improvement in real throughput.
  • Paged attention — allocate the KV cache in fixed-size blocks like OS virtual memory instead of one contiguous reservation per sequence. Kills the internal fragmentation caused by reserving for max context, and lets sequences with a shared prefix (a long system prompt) share physical blocks.
  • Chunked prefill — the simpler alternative to full disaggregation, and what most single-cluster deployments actually run. Split a long prefill into chunks and mix them into the same batch as in-flight decode tokens, so one long prompt can't stall every generating sequence. Chunk size trades time-to-first-token against inter-token latency, and it is the default in modern serving engines.
  • Prefill/decode disaggregation — prefill is compute-bound and parallel over the prompt; decode is memory-bound and sequential. They have opposite hardware profiles, so running them in one pool means one of them is always starving the other. Split the pools and you can size and autoscale each correctly.
  • Speculative decoding — a cheap drafter proposes several tokens, the large model verifies them all in one forward pass, and accepted tokens come nearly free. Two things to get right, because both are commonly overstated. First, rejection sampling preserves the target model's output distribution, not its exact output — token-identical only at temperature 0. Second, it is not safe to enable everywhere: it trades spare compute for latency, so at high batch sizes, where continuous batching has already saturated the GPU, verifying rejected drafts burns FLOPs for nothing and reduces aggregate throughput. Real stacks gate it on queue depth. Reported speedups fall from roughly 2× at batch 1 to about 1.2× at batch 128. Modern variants (EAGLE, Medusa, or plain n-gram / prompt-lookup drafting) are self-speculative and need no second model, which is why most teams use those rather than maintaining a separate draft model.

The platform around the model

Routing: classify difficulty and send most traffic to a small model, escalating only when needed. Multi-tenancy: per-team token quotas with a token-bucket limiter (§11) — GPU time is the scarce resource, so rate limit in tokens, not requests. Caching: prefix-cache long shared system prompts so prefill is not re-paid on every call. Autoscaling: scale on queue depth and time-to-first-token, not CPU; and cold-starting a GPU node means loading 35 GB of weights, so keep a warm pool — a 90-second scale-up is useless during a spike. Fallbacks: a queue with an admission deadline, then load-shed with a clear error, then a smaller model as the degraded tier.

Three more prompts, sketched

Ad click prediction

The twist: calibration, not ranking — the score is multiplied by a bid, so it must be a true probability. Use log loss; check a calibration plot; recalibrate with isotonic regression after any negative downsampling (and correct the intercept for the sampling rate). Latency budget is ~10 ms inside the auction. Features are enormously sparse and high-cardinality → hashing trick plus learned embeddings.

Fraud / abuse detection

The twist: extreme imbalance (~0.1%), adversarial drift, and a 30-day label delay from chargebacks. Optimize PR-AUC. Pick the threshold from the cost matrix (a blocked good customer costs more than you think). Combine rules (instant, auditable, no training) with a model. Graph features — shared devices, IPs, payment instruments — carry most of the signal.

Content moderation

The twist: two thresholds, not one — auto-remove above the high one, auto-allow below the low one, and route the band between to human review. Design the review queue as part of the system, sized by capacity, and treat its output as your highest-quality labels. Multi-modal, multilingual, and precision is a policy decision made by people, not by you.

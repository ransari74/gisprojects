LLM & agent systems

The material that has appeared in loops most recently, and where the bar is least settled. Two things carry a round here: arithmetic you can do on the whiteboard, and knowing which published numbers are measurements and which are marketing. The second is rarer and lands harder.

How to discuss engine benchmarks without getting caught

Published head-to-heads put compiled TensorRT-LLM modestly ahead of vLLM, with SGLang pulling ahead as prefix sharing rises — but the specific percentages come from blog comparisons rather than controlled measurement, and they move between posts. The defensible statement is directional: "the gaps are tens of percent, not multiples, and they invert depending on how much prefix our workload shares — I'd benchmark on our own traffic." A candidate who quotes a precise multiple from memory is telling the interviewer they read a blog. A candidate who names the workload property that decides it is telling them they've served a model.

Three structural facts to pull out of that table, because they are the interesting part:

  • MoE changes compute per token, not KV cache. Mixtral 8×7B has 47B total parameters and exactly the same KV cache per token as Llama 3.1 8B, because the cache depends only on layers × KV heads × head dim. "MoE needs more KV memory" is a sentence that means someone hasn't done the arithmetic.
  • GQA is a 4–8× cut versus MHA at the same layer count — 512 KB/token for MHA-7B against 128 KB/token for GQA-8B.
  • MLA compresses what is stored rather than reducing how many heads exist. DeepSeek-V3 caches one shared low-rank latent (512) plus a small decoupled RoPE key (64) per layer, with no ×2 for K and V — roughly 98% smaller than same-shape MHA. The famous "93.3% reduction" figure is against DeepSeek's previous-generation dense model, not against same-shape MHA; being able to state that distinction is a real signal. The cost is extra FLOPs and an awkward interaction with tensor parallelism, which is why GQA remains the industry default.

Halve the marketing number

NVIDIA routinely quotes tensor-core throughput with 2:4 structured sparsity — H100 "1,979 TFLOP/s BF16", B200 "18 PFLOP/s FP4". No production LLM inference uses 2:4 sparsity, so the dense figure is half of whatever is on the slide. B200 memory and bandwidth also genuinely differ across NVIDIA's own materials (180 vs 192 GB, 7.7 vs 8.0 TB/s) because one figure is software-visible and the other physical. Saying "dense, not sparse" costs two words and marks you as someone who has sized a cluster.

The five throughput techniques, with their real provenance

TechniqueMechanismReported gain — and against what
Paged attentionKV cache in fixed-size blocks (~16 tokens) with a per-sequence block table, exactly like OS virtual memory. Blocks allocate on demand; shared prefixes share physical blocks copy-on-write.Cuts KV memory waste from 60–80% down to under 4%. The often-quoted "2–4× throughput" is the original paper against 2023-vintage baselines — stale as a claim about modern engines.
Continuous batchingBatch composition re-decided every decode step: finished sequences evicted, queued ones admitted mid-flight. No padding, no waiting for the slowest generation.~4× against competent static batching; the widely-repeated "23×" is against naive single-request serving. The gain scales with variance in output length — uniform outputs gain nothing, chat and agents gain multiples.
Chunked prefillSplit a long prefill into chunks and mix them with decode tokens in one batch, so a long prompt can't stall every generating sequence.Up to 5.6× serving capacity at a fixed latency SLO. Chunk size trades time-to-first-token against inter-token latency.
Prefix cachingHash block-aligned prefixes and reuse their KV blocks; a radix tree generalizes it to partial matches.Hit rates of 50–99% depending on workload, highest for agents sharing a system prompt and tool schema. The commercial analogue is API prompt caching, where cached reads are heavily discounted.
PD disaggregationPrefill (compute-bound, bursty) and decode (bandwidth-bound, long-running) on separate GPU pools, KV cache shipped over NVLink/RDMA.~2× goodput — requests meeting both TTFT and TPOT targets — by removing phase interference. Only pays when the interconnect is fast and prompts are long enough that transfer cost is small next to prefill.

Name TTFT and TPOT explicitly. Time-to-first-token is prefill, and is what a user feels as responsiveness. Time-per-output-token is decode, and is what they feel as reading speed. Aggregate throughput is what the finance team feels. Those three trade against each other, and every serving decision moves at least two of them — a candidate who says only "throughput" has one dimension where the problem has three.

Quantization, with what actually degrades

MethodQuantizesNotes
FP8 (E4M3)Weights + activations, native on Hopper/BlackwellThe production default now: ~2× memory, ~1.6× throughput, minimal accuracy impact, and FP8 KV caches report over 99% accuracy recovery. Most of these figures come from vendors who ship the tooling — reproducible, but not third-party audited.

QLoRA / NF44-bit frozen base + BF16 LoRA adaptersA training technique, not a serving one. Takes 65B finetuning from over 780 GB to under 48 GB — one GPU — with no measured degradation versus 16-bit finetuning.

What breaks first is not the average benchmark score: it is outlier activations, long-context coherence, and rare-token or non-English performance. A model can hold its average while one slice falls apart. So: quantize, then evaluate on your own task — and remember that a quantized larger model generally beats a full-precision smaller one at the same memory budget, which is usually the actual decision in front of you.

Agentic systems

"Design an agent that can take actions on a user's account" is now a standard prompt. It is a systems question about blast radius, budgets, and evaluation — not a prompt-engineering question.

Tool calling and the loop

  • Tool schemas are the design. Few, orthogonal, well-named tools with tight JSON Schemas beat many overlapping ones. Descriptions are prompt real estate — write them for the model, not as API reference docs.
  • Tool results are the hidden cost. A 50k-token tool output blows the context and poisons everything downstream. Paginate, truncate with a continuation token, or write to a file and return a handle.
  • Errors must be recoverable. Return an actionable message the model can act on, not a stack trace.
  • ReAct (thought → action → observation) is what most production agents actually are. Plan-and-execute is cheaper and more auditable but brittle when the environment surprises it. Reflection helps most when there is a real verifier — tests, a compiler, a schema — rather than the model grading itself.
  • Budgets belong in the harness, not the prompt. Max steps, max tokens, max wall-clock, max spend. A prompt that says "be efficient" is not a budget.

Memory — four kinds, and say which you need

Working
The context window itself, managed by compaction and summarization as it fills.
Episodic
What happened in past sessions — transcripts and summaries, retrieved on demand.
Semantic
Durable extracted facts about the user or domain, written to a store.
Procedural
Learned skills and instructions, persisted as files or system-prompt fragments.

Current practice favours files and structured stores over vector memory for agents: a scratchpad the agent reads and writes is more debuggable, more editable, and less lossy than embedding-based recall. The hard problems to name are what to write (over-writing pollutes the store), invalidation, and conflicting memories.

Multi-agent — and the finding that should make you cautious

The orchestrator–worker pattern — a lead decomposes the task, subagents work in parallel with isolated context windows, the lead synthesizes — has the best-documented production result available: a published research system reported roughly 90% better performance than a single-agent baseline on its internal eval, at about 15× the tokens, and, crucially, that token usage alone explained around 80% of the performance variance.

That last number is the one to quote, because it deflates the mystique: most of the multi-agent gain is buying more total inference and more independent context windows, not emergent coordination. Which gives a clean decision rule — multi-agent pays when the task is breadth-first, with many independent search paths whose combined information exceeds one context window, and the outcome is worth a 15× token bill. It does not pay for narrow, sequential, or latency-sensitive work.

Sandboxing, and the lethal trifecta

Any agent that executes code or touches a filesystem needs a real isolation boundary (a microVM or sandboxed container, not a subprocess), egress allowlisting, filesystem scoping, resource limits, no ambient credentials — short-lived scoped tokens injected per task — and human approval gates on irreversible actions.

The framing that wins this part of the round

An agent is dangerous when it has all three of: access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and a channel to send data out. Any two are manageable; all three is not. Egress allowlisting is how you break the third leg, which is why it does more for agent safety than any amount of prompt hardening. State the design principle plainly: make the blast radius small enough that you can afford for the agent to be wrong — because it will be.

The tool-connection layer has its own threat model worth naming: tool poisoning (malicious instructions hidden in a tool's description), rug-pull redefinition after a user has approved a tool, cross-server shadowing, injection via tool results, and credential passthrough. Standardization work on tool protocols has moved toward a stateless core precisely so that gateways can inspect and enforce policy on every request rather than trusting a session.

Evaluating agents — trajectory, not just the answer

The single most useful metric is pass^k: the probability that all k independent attempts succeed. An agent with 60% pass@1 and 5% pass^8 is unshippable, and only the second number tells you that. Alongside it: cost per task, steps per task, tool-call error rate, recovery rate after a failed call, and variance across seeds — agents are far higher-variance than single-turn models.

Benchmarks worth knowing by name: SWE-bench Verified (real GitHub issues graded by the repository's own tests), τ²-bench (tool-agent-user interaction scoring policy-compliant completion and correct refusal), GAIA, OSWorld and WebArena for computer and browser use, and time-horizon measurements asking how long a task an agent completes at 50% reliability.

On failure modes, the useful research finding is that validated taxonomies of multi-agent failure — specification ambiguity, inter-agent coordination breakdown, verification gaps — identify failures that are not detectable at the individual-agent level. A collection of safe agents is not a safe collection of agents. That sentence is worth having ready.

Training at scale — the one-line rules

ML-systems loops ask about training infrastructure at least as often as serving. You do not need to have run a thousand-GPU job; you need to know which technique solves which shortage.

The composition order they expect: TP within a node (over NVLink) → PP across nodes (point-to-point rather than collective, so it tolerates slower links) → data/FSDP across what's left → sequence parallelism layered onto TP for long context → expert parallelism for MoE. The reason TP stays intra-node is exactly those two all-reduces per layer: push them over InfiniBand instead of NVLink and throughput collapses.

Memory accounting, worth reciting

Per parameter, a training step holds 2 bytes (BF16 weights) + 2–4 (gradients) + 8 (Adam's fp32 m and v) + 4 (fp32 master weights) ≈ 16–18 bytes per parameter, before activations. So a 7B model is ~120 GB of static state — which is why a model that infers comfortably on one 24 GB card cannot be full-finetuned on eight of them without sharding, and why ZeRO exists. Say this and half the follow-ups answer themselves.

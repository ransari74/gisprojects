The LeetCode ladder

175 problems, organized by pattern rather than by difficulty, each list ordered from most foundational to hardest. Every number and title here was verified against a full problem index rather than copied from a community list — several popular mirrors contain wrong numbers, and a wrong number in a study plan costs you an evening.

Weakest pedagogy of the four.

The list below is a merge, pattern-blocked in the NeetCode style, with the Top Interview 150's practical additions folded in. If you have less than four weeks, do only the bolded problems — 48 of them, two or three per pattern, chosen because each teaches one idea the rest of its group reuses.

How to work the ladder

Do a pattern in a block of five to seven problems — that's what builds the template into your fingers. Then interleave on review days: shuffle problems from different patterns, because in the real interview nobody tells you which pattern it is, and classifying it is the actual skill being tested. Rate every attempt and follow the spacing schedule in §07; an untracked ladder becomes a list of problems you once saw.

Mapping the ladder onto the 28 days

The realistic rate is 4–5 problems in a 75-minute block if you're doing them properly — attempt, study, note, and re-implement the previous day's misses. Six a day is a 4–6 hour commitment, which is a full-time schedule; the honest version of this plan is below.

That's roughly 110 problems in 28 days — the bolded core plus the connective tissue — with the remaining 65 as the stretch set. If you have two hours a day rather than three, run the same order over eight weeks at three a day and keep every review day. Do not compress by deleting the review days; that is the single most common way this plan fails, and it fails quietly, because you'll feel productive right up until the interview.

On problem counts, honestly

The recurring story on interview forums is someone who solved 250–300 problems and still failed, then passed after a smaller number done deliberately with mocks and re-solves. The mechanism isn't mysterious: interviews test whether you can classify an unfamiliar problem and communicate a solution under time pressure, and raw problem count trains neither. Budget at least a quarter of your algorithm hours for re-solves and mock conditions rather than new problems. And if you are starting from genuinely rusty fundamentals, be honest that this is a 3–6 month project rather than a 28-day one — the 28-day plan assumes you can already write a BFS without looking it up.

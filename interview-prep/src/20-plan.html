The 28-day calendar

Built for roughly 3 hours on weekdays and 5 on weekends. Before you commit to it, read the next two paragraphs honestly — the plan works for one specific starting point, and pretending you're at that point is the most expensive mistake available.

Is 28 days the right number for you?

This plan assumes you can already write a BFS, a binary search, and a hash-map pass without looking anything up. If you can, four weeks of concentrated work is genuinely enough to sharpen design and ML systems and get your recall back. If you can't — if the templates in §04 are new rather than rusty — then this is a 10–14 week project, and compressing it produces a confident-feeling candidate who fails the coding round. There is no shame in the longer timeline; there is real cost in discovering it during a loop. Run the same order at a slower cadence and keep every review day.

Which track are you on?

Rusty on algorithms

You've been doing product work for years and haven't written a graph traversal since university. Start at §03 (complexity), then live in §04–§07 for six weeks before you touch design. Your bottleneck is fluency, and fluency only comes from timed repetition.

Strong coder, weak on design

The most common case, and the one this 28-day plan is built for. Keep algorithms on maintenance (§07), and spend the recovered hours in §09–§13. Your gap is almost certainly driving the round rather than knowledge — see §20 on what changes between mid and senior.

Targeting ML or AI roles

Do not skip Part III. An ML design round is a distributed-systems round with two extra loops, and candidates who only know modeling get exposed in the first deep dive. Weeks 1–2 as written, then §14–§17 in full.

The rule that makes this work

Reading a design is worth about a fifth of drawing one. Every topic day ends with fifteen minutes at a blank page: redraw the day's architecture from memory, speaking it aloud as you draw, then diff it against the section. The diff is your gap list, and the gap list is what day 25 is for. The mouth is the muscle being trained here, not the hand — the first time you narrate a design should not be in an interview.
